\label{prob:medical-test}
A medical test finds a serious disease in a patient. The test is 99\%
accurate: if the patient has the disease, the test is positive with
probability 99\%; if the patient does not have the disease, the test is
negative with probability 99\%. The disease is rare: only one person in
10\,000 has it. Find the probability that the patient really has the
disease.

\begin{solution}
\solpart{Interpretation}
Let $D \in \{0, 1\}$ show whether the patient has the disease ($D = 1$).
Let $T \in \{0, 1\}$ be the test result ($T = 1$ is a positive result).
The problem gives three values:
\[
  p(D = 1) = 10^{-4}, \qquad
  p(T = 1 \mid D = 1) = 0.99, \qquad
  p(T = 0 \mid D = 0) = 0.99.
\]
Find $p(D = 1 \mid T = 1)$.

\solpart{Idea}
The disease causes the test result, so the causal graph is $D \to T$.
The graph gives the joint distribution as a product of two factors:
\[
  p(D, T) = p(D) \, p(T \mid D).
\]
The problem gives both factors: the distribution of the source node $D$
and the conditional distribution on the edge $D \to T$. Thus the joint
distribution is known. From the joint distribution, you can get any other
probability with two operations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A marginal probability is the sum of the joint probability over
    the other variables:
    \[
      p(T) = \sum_{D} p(D, T).
    \]
    For continuous variables $x$ and $y$, the sum is an integral:
    $p(y) = \int p(x, y) \, dx$.
  \item A conditional probability is a joint probability divided by a
    marginal probability:
    \[
      p(D \mid T) = \frac{p(D, T)}{p(T)}.
    \]
\end{itemize}
These two operations together give Bayes' theorem:
\[
  p(D \mid T)
  = \frac{p(D, T)}{p(T)}
  = \frac{p(D, T)}{\sum_{D'} p(D', T)}
  = \frac{p(D) \, p(T \mid D)}{\sum_{D'} p(D') \, p(T \mid D')}.
\]

\solpart{Solution}
\solstep{Step 1. Find the missing probabilities.}
The probabilities of the two values of a variable add to 1. Thus
\begin{align*}
  p(D = 0) &= 1 - p(D = 1) = 1 - 0.0001 = 0.9999, \\
  p(T = 1 \mid D = 0) &= 1 - p(T = 0 \mid D = 0) = 1 - 0.99 = 0.01.
\end{align*}
The second value is the probability of a false positive result.

\solstep{Step 2. Find the joint probabilities of a positive result.}
Use $p(D, T) = p(D) \, p(T \mid D)$:
\begin{align*}
  p(D = 1, T = 1) &= p(D = 1) \, p(T = 1 \mid D = 1)
    = 0.0001 \cdot 0.99 = 0.000099, \\
  p(D = 0, T = 1) &= p(D = 0) \, p(T = 1 \mid D = 0)
    = 0.9999 \cdot 0.01 = 0.009999.
\end{align*}

\solstep{Step 3. Find the marginal probability of a positive result.}
Sum the joint probabilities over $D$:
\[
  p(T = 1)
  = p(D = 1, T = 1) + p(D = 0, T = 1)
  = 0.000099 + 0.009999
  = 0.010098.
\]

\solstep{Step 4. Divide the joint probability by the marginal probability.}
\[
  p(D = 1 \mid T = 1)
  = \frac{p(D = 1, T = 1)}{p(T = 1)}
  = \frac{0.000099}{0.010098}
  = \frac{99}{10098}
  = \frac{99}{99 \cdot 102}
  = \frac{1}{102}
  \approx 0.0098.
\]
The patient has the disease with a probability of approximately 1\%.

\solpart{Conclusion}
A positive result increases the probability of the disease from 0.01\% to
approximately 1\%. But the patient is still healthy with a probability of
99\%. The cause is the low prior probability. In 10\,000 people, the test
finds approximately $10\,000 \cdot 0.000099 \approx 1$ sick person and
$10\,000 \cdot 0.009999 \approx 100$ healthy people. For this reason, most
positive results of a mass test for a rare disease are false.

A second independent test gives more information. After the first positive
result, the probability of the disease is $1/102$. This value is the prior
probability for the second test. If the second result is also positive, then
\[
  p(D = 1 \mid T_1 = 1, T_2 = 1)
  = \frac{\frac{1}{102} \cdot 0.99}
         {\frac{1}{102} \cdot 0.99 + \frac{101}{102} \cdot 0.01}
  = \frac{0.99}{0.99 + 1.01}
  = 0.495.
\]
\end{solution}

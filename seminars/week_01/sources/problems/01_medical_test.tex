\label{prob:medical-test}
A medical test finds a serious disease in a patient. The test is 99\%
accurate: if the patient has the disease, the test is positive with
probability 99\%; if the patient does not have the disease, the test is
negative with probability 99\%. The disease is rare: only one person in
10\,000 has it. Find the probability that the patient really has the
disease.

\begin{solution}
\solpart{Interpretation}
$D = 1$: the patient has the disease. $T = 1$: the test result is positive.
\[
  p(D = 1) = 10^{-4}, \qquad
  p(T = 1 \mid D = 1) = 0.99, \qquad
  p(T = 0 \mid D = 0) = 0.99.
\]
Find $p(D = 1 \mid T = 1)$.

\solpart{Idea}
The disease causes the test result. The causal graph has one arrow:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      node distance=1.5cm, >={Stealth},
      var/.style={circle, draw, minimum size=0.75cm},
    ]
    \node[var] (D) {$D$};
    \node[var, right=of D] (T) {$T$};
    \draw[->] (D) -- (T);
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
Plan for a conditional probability of query variables $X$ given observed
variables $Y$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Write the joint distribution of all variables with the chain rule
    and the graph.
  \item Write the goal as a ratio:
    $p(X \mid Y) = \dfrac{p(X, Y)}{p(Y)}$.
  \item Find the numerator and the denominator as sums of the joint
    distribution over the other variables:
    $p(X, Y) = \sum_{z} p(X, Y, Z = z)$ and $p(Y) = \sum_{x} p(X = x, Y)$.
    Move the factors that do not depend on the summation variable out of
    the sum. A distribution sums to 1 over all its values.
\end{enumerate}

\solpart{Solution}
\solstep{Step 1. Write the joint distribution.}
\[
  p(D, T) = p(D) \, p(T \mid D)
  \qquad \text{(chain rule; parent of $T$: $D$)}.
\]

\solstep{Step 2. Write the goal as a ratio.}
\[
  p(D = 1 \mid T = 1) = \frac{p(D = 1, T = 1)}{p(T = 1)}.
\]

\solstep{Step 3. Find the numerator.}
All variables are fixed, so there is no sum:
\[
  p(D = 1, T = 1)
  = p(D = 1) \, p(T = 1 \mid D = 1)
  = 0.0001 \cdot 0.99
  = 0.000099.
\]

\solstep{Step 4. Find the denominator.}
Sum the joint distribution over $D$:
\begin{align*}
  p(T = 1)
  &= \sum_{d} p(D = d) \, p(T = 1 \mid D = d) \\
  &= p(D = 1) \, p(T = 1 \mid D = 1) + p(D = 0) \, p(T = 1 \mid D = 0)
    && \text{($d \in \{0, 1\}$)} \\
  &= 0.000099
   + \bigl(1 - p(D = 1)\bigr) \bigl(1 - p(T = 0 \mid D = 0)\bigr)
    && \text{(Step 3; complements)} \\
  &= 0.000099 + (1 - 0.0001) \cdot (1 - 0.99) \\
  &= 0.000099 + 0.9999 \cdot 0.01 \\
  &= 0.000099 + 0.009999
   = 0.010098.
\end{align*}

\solstep{Step 5. Divide.}
\begin{align*}
  p(D = 1 \mid T = 1)
  &= \frac{0.000099}{0.010098}
    && \text{(Steps 3, 4)} \\
  &= \frac{99}{10098}
    && \text{(multiply by $10^6$)} \\
  &= \frac{99}{99 \cdot 102}
    && \text{($10098 = 99 \cdot 102$)} \\
  &= \frac{1}{102}
   \approx 0.0098.
\end{align*}

\solpart{Conclusion}
The probability increases from 0.01\% to only 1\%. In 10\,000 people, the
test finds $10\,000 \cdot 0.000099 \approx 1$ sick person and
$10\,000 \cdot 0.009999 \approx 100$ healthy people. Thus most positive
results of a mass test for a rare disease are false. A second positive
independent test uses $p(D = 1 \mid T_1 = 1) = 1/102$ as the prior:
\begin{align*}
  p(D = 1 \mid T_1 = 1, T_2 = 1)
  &= \frac{\frac{1}{102} \cdot 0.99}
          {\frac{1}{102} \cdot 0.99 + \bigl(1 - \frac{1}{102}\bigr) \cdot 0.01}
    && \text{(Bayes' theorem)} \\
  &= \frac{\frac{1}{102} \cdot 0.99}
          {\frac{1}{102} \cdot 0.99 + \frac{101}{102} \cdot 0.01}
    && \textstyle\text{($1 - \frac{1}{102} = \frac{101}{102}$)} \\
  &= \frac{0.99}{0.99 + 101 \cdot 0.01}
    && \text{(multiply by 102)} \\
  &= \frac{0.99}{0.99 + 1.01}
   = \frac{0.99}{2}
   = 0.495.
\end{align*}
\end{solution}

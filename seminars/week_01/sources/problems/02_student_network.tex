\label{prob:student-network}
Consider the following probabilistic model. At a seminar, a student answers
badly ($A = 1$) or well ($A = 0$). This depends on whether the student has
depression ($D = 1$ or $D = 0$) and whether the student went to a party the
night before ($P = 1$ or $P = 0$). The party can also give the student a
headache ($H = 1$). A bad answer makes the teacher unhappy ($T = 1$).
Figure~\ref{fig:student-network} shows the causal relations in the model.
The probabilities are:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}[t]{c|cc}
    \toprule
    $p(A = 1 \mid P, D)$ & $P$ & $D$ \\
    \midrule
    0.999 & 1 & 1 \\
    0.9   & 1 & 0 \\
    0.9   & 0 & 1 \\
    0.01  & 0 & 0 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \quad
  \begin{tabular}[t]{c|c}
    \toprule
    $p(H = 1 \mid P)$ & $P$ \\
    \midrule
    0.9 & 1 \\
    0.2 & 0 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \quad
  \begin{tabular}[t]{c|c}
    \toprule
    $p(T = 1 \mid A)$ & $A$ \\
    \midrule
    0.95 & 1 \\
    0.5  & 0 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \medskip
  $p(P = 1) = 0.2, \qquad p(D = 1) = 0.4.$
\end{center}
Find $p(P = 1 \mid H = 1)$, $p(P = 1 \mid T = 1)$, and
$p(P = 1 \mid T = 1, H = 1)$.

  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      node distance=0.3cm and 0.5cm,
      var/.style={circle, draw, minimum size=0.75cm},
      >={Stealth},

    \node[var] (P) {$P$};
    \node[var, right=2cm of P] (D) {$D$};
    \node[var, below left=of P] (H) {$H$};
    \node[var, below right=of P] (A) {$A$};
    \node[var, below=of A] (T) {$T$};
    \draw[->] (P) -- (H);
    \draw[->] (P) -- (A);

    \draw[->] (A) -- (T);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Causal relations for Problem~\ref{prob:student-network}}
  \label{fig:student-network}
\end{figure}

\begin{solution}
\solpart{Idea}
This problem uses the method of Problem~\ref{prob:medical-test} on a
larger graph. The graph in Figure~\ref{fig:student-network} gives the joint
distribution as a product of one factor for each node. Each factor is the
distribution of the node given its parents:
\[
  p(P, D, A, H, T)
  = p(P) \, p(D) \, p(A \mid P, D) \, p(H \mid P) \, p(T \mid A).
\]
The tables give all five factors. Thus the joint distribution is known,
and you can get any probability from it. To find the probability of $P$
given an observation, move through the graph in two directions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Along the edges, from $P$ to the observed variable. Sum over each
    unobserved variable on the path, for example
    $p(T \mid P) = \sum_A p(T \mid A) \, p(A \mid P)$.
  \item Against the edges, from the observed variable back to $P$. Use
    Bayes' theorem, for example
    $p(P \mid T) = p(P) \, p(T \mid P) / p(T)$.
\end{enumerate}
Each factor $p(\text{node} \mid \text{parents})$ sums to 1 over the values of
the node. Thus, when you sum the joint distribution over a variable that is
not a parent of other variables, its factor disappears.

\solpart{Solution}
\solstep{Step 1. Find the missing probabilities.}
The probabilities of the two values of a variable add to 1:
\[
  p(P = 0) = 1 - 0.2 = 0.8, \qquad
  p(D = 0) = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6.
\]

\solstep{Step 2. Show that $P$ and $D$ are independent.}
Sum the joint distribution over $T$, then $H$, then $A$. Each sum removes
one factor, because the factor sums to 1:
\[
  p(P, D)
  = \sum_{A} \sum_{H} \sum_{T} p(P, D, A, H, T)
  = p(P) \, p(D).
\]
Thus $p(D \mid P) = p(P, D) / p(P) = p(D)$.

\solstep{Step 3. Write $p(A \mid P)$ as a sum over $D$.}
The depression $D$ is not observed. Sum over $D$, then use the definition
of a conditional probability, then use Step~2:
\[
  p(A \mid P)
  = \sum_{D} p(A, D \mid P)
  = \sum_{D} p(A \mid P, D) \, p(D \mid P)
  = \sum_{D} p(A \mid P, D) \, p(D).
\]

\solstep{Step 4. Calculate $p(A = 1 \mid P)$.}
Substitute the values from the tables and from Step~1:
\begin{align*}
  p(A = 1 \mid P = 1)
    &= 0.999 \cdot 0.4 + 0.9 \cdot 0.6
     = 0.3996 + 0.54
     = 0.9396, \\
  p(A = 1 \mid P = 0)
    &= 0.9 \cdot 0.4 + 0.01 \cdot 0.6
     = 0.36 + 0.006
     = 0.366.
\end{align*}
Thus $p(A = 0 \mid P = 1) = 1 - 0.9396 = 0.0604$ and
$p(A = 0 \mid P = 0) = 1 - 0.366 = 0.634$.

\solstep{Step 5. Show that $p(T \mid A, P) = p(T \mid A)$.}
Sum the joint distribution over $H$ and $D$. Then do the same without $T$:
\begin{align*}
  p(P, A, T) &= p(P) \, p(T \mid A) \sum_{D} p(D) \, p(A \mid P, D), \\
  p(P, A) &= p(P) \sum_{D} p(D) \, p(A \mid P, D).
\end{align*}
Divide the first line by the second line:
$p(T \mid A, P) = p(P, A, T) / p(P, A) = p(T \mid A)$.

\solstep{Step 6. Write $p(T \mid P)$ as a sum over $A$.}
The answer $A$ is not observed. Sum over $A$, then use the definition of a
conditional probability, then use Step~5:
\[
  p(T \mid P)
  = \sum_{A} p(T, A \mid P)
  = \sum_{A} p(T \mid A, P) \, p(A \mid P)
  = \sum_{A} p(T \mid A) \, p(A \mid P).
\]

\solstep{Step 7. Calculate $p(T = 1 \mid P)$.}
Substitute the values from the table and from Step~4:
\begin{align*}
  p(T = 1 \mid P = 1)
    &= 0.95 \cdot 0.9396 + 0.5 \cdot 0.0604
     = 0.89262 + 0.0302
     = 0.92282, \\
  p(T = 1 \mid P = 0)
    &= 0.95 \cdot 0.366 + 0.5 \cdot 0.634
     = 0.3477 + 0.317
     = 0.6647.
\end{align*}

\solstep{Step 8. Find $p(P = 1 \mid H = 1)$.}
Sum the joint distribution over $T$, $A$, and $D$. Each sum removes one
factor, so $p(P, H) = p(P) \, p(H \mid P)$. Calculate the joint
probabilities:
\begin{align*}
  p(P = 1, H = 1) &= 0.2 \cdot 0.9 = 0.18, \\
  p(P = 0, H = 1) &= 0.8 \cdot 0.2 = 0.16.
\end{align*}
Sum them over $P$: $p(H = 1) = 0.18 + 0.16 = 0.34$. Divide:
\[
  p(P = 1 \mid H = 1)
  = \frac{p(P = 1, H = 1)}{p(H = 1)}
  = \frac{0.18}{0.34}
  = \frac{9}{17}
  \approx 0.529.
\]

\solstep{Step 9. Find $p(P = 1 \mid T = 1)$.}
Use $p(P, T) = p(P) \, p(T \mid P)$ and the values from Step~7:
\begin{align*}
  p(P = 1, T = 1) &= 0.2 \cdot 0.92282 = 0.184564, \\
  p(P = 0, T = 1) &= 0.8 \cdot 0.6647 = 0.53176.
\end{align*}
Sum them over $P$: $p(T = 1) = 0.184564 + 0.53176 = 0.716324$. Divide:
\[
  p(P = 1 \mid T = 1)
  = \frac{p(P = 1, T = 1)}{p(T = 1)}
  = \frac{0.184564}{0.716324}
  \approx 0.258.
\]

\solstep{Step 10. Show that $H$ and $T$ are independent given $P$.}
Sum the joint distribution over the unobserved variables $D$ and $A$:
\begin{align*}
  p(P, H, T)
  &= \sum_{A} \sum_{D} p(P) \, p(D) \, p(A \mid P, D) \, p(H \mid P) \,
     p(T \mid A) \\
  &= p(P) \, p(H \mid P) \sum_{A} p(T \mid A) \sum_{D} p(D) \,
     p(A \mid P, D)
     && \text{(move factors out of the sums)} \\
  &= p(P) \, p(H \mid P) \sum_{A} p(T \mid A) \, p(A \mid P)
     && \text{(Step 3)} \\
  &= p(P) \, p(H \mid P) \, p(T \mid P)
     && \text{(Step 6)}.
\end{align*}
Divide by $p(P)$: $p(H, T \mid P) = p(H \mid P) \, p(T \mid P)$.

\solstep{Step 11. Find $p(P = 1 \mid T = 1, H = 1)$.}
Use the product from Step~10 and the values from Step~7:
\begin{align*}
  p(P = 1, H = 1, T = 1) &= 0.2 \cdot 0.9 \cdot 0.92282 = 0.166108, \\
  p(P = 0, H = 1, T = 1) &= 0.8 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 0.6647 = 0.106352.
\end{align*}
Sum them over $P$: $p(H = 1, T = 1) = 0.166108 + 0.106352 = 0.27246$.
Divide:
\[
  p(P = 1 \mid T = 1, H = 1)
  = \frac{p(P = 1, H = 1, T = 1)}{p(H = 1, T = 1)}
  = \frac{0.166108}{0.27246}
  \approx 0.610.
\]

\solpart{Conclusion}
The prior probability of the party is 0.2. A headache increases it to
0.529. An unhappy teacher increases it only to 0.258, because a bad answer
has other causes, and the teacher is often unhappy without a bad answer.
The two observations together give the highest probability, 0.610.
\end{solution}

\label{prob:student-network}
Consider the following probabilistic model. At a seminar, a student answers
badly ($A = 1$) or well ($A = 0$). This depends on whether the student has
depression ($D = 1$ or $D = 0$) and whether the student went to a party the
night before ($P = 1$ or $P = 0$). The party can also give the student a
headache ($H = 1$). A bad answer makes the teacher unhappy ($T = 1$).
Figure~\ref{fig:student-network} shows the causal relations in the model.
The probabilities are:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}[t]{c|cc}
    \toprule
    $p(A = 1 \mid P, D)$ & $P$ & $D$ \\
    \midrule
    0.999 & 1 & 1 \\
    0.9   & 1 & 0 \\
    0.9   & 0 & 1 \\
    0.01  & 0 & 0 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \quad
  \begin{tabular}[t]{c|c}
    \toprule
    $p(H = 1 \mid P)$ & $P$ \\
    \midrule
    0.9 & 1 \\
    0.2 & 0 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \quad
  \begin{tabular}[t]{c|c}
    \toprule
    $p(T = 1 \mid A)$ & $A$ \\
    \midrule
    0.95 & 1 \\
    0.5  & 0 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \medskip
  $p(P = 1) = 0.2, \qquad p(D = 1) = 0.4.$
\end{center}
Find $p(P = 1 \mid H = 1)$, $p(P = 1 \mid T = 1)$, and
$p(P = 1 \mid T = 1, H = 1)$.

\begin{solution}
\solpart{Idea}
Problem~\ref{prob:medical-test} is a special case of this method.

\emph{Joint distribution.} The problem gives the distribution of each source
node and the distribution of each other node given its parents. The chain
rule, from the children to the parents, gives the joint distribution as the
product of these factors. Example for the graph $X \to Z \leftarrow Y$:
\begin{align*}
  p(X, Y, Z)
  &= p(X, Y) \, p(Z \mid X, Y)
    && \text{(chain rule)} \\
  &= p(X) \, p(Y \mid X) \, p(Z \mid X, Y)
    && \text{(chain rule)} \\
  &= p(X) \, p(Y) \, p(Z \mid X, Y)
    && \text{($Y$ has no parents)}.
\end{align*}

\emph{Algorithm for $p(G)$}, where $G$ is a group of variables with fixed
values:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item $\Ganc$: the ancestors of $G$ that are not in $G$. $\Grest$: all
    other variables.
  \item $p(G) = \sum_{\Ganc} p(G, \Ganc)$: the sum over $\Grest$ is 1.
  \item $G$ and $\Ganc$ together contain the parents of all their variables.
    Thus the chain rule gives $p(G, \Ganc)$ as a product of the given
    tables.
  \item Move the factors that do not depend on a summation variable out of
    its sum. Then calculate.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Proof of Steps 2 and 3.} No arrow goes from $\Grest$ to $G$ or
$\Ganc$: otherwise, its start is an ancestor of $G$. Thus the parents of
each variable in $G \cup \Ganc$ are in $G \cup \Ganc$:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      >={Stealth},
      dot/.style={circle, draw, fill=white, minimum size=0.3cm, inner sep=0pt},
      box/.style={draw, rounded corners, inner sep=7pt},
      meta/.style={->, very thick},
    ]
    \node[dot] (a1) at (0, 0) {};
    \node[dot] (a2) at (1, -0.5) {};
    \node[dot] (a3) at (2, 0) {};
    \draw[->] (a1) -- (a2);
    \draw[->] (a3) -- (a2);
    \node[dot] (g1) at (-2, -2.4) {};
    \node[dot] (g2) at (-1, -2.9) {};
    \node[dot] (g3) at (-2, -3.4) {};
    \draw[->] (g1) -- (g2);
    \draw[->] (g3) -- (g2);
    \node[dot] (r1) at (3, -2.4) {};
    \node[dot] (r2) at (4, -2.9) {};
    \node[dot] (r3) at (3, -3.4) {};
    \draw[->] (r1) -- (r2);
    \draw[->] (r2) -- (r3);
    \begin{scope}[on background layer]
      \node[box, anc, fit=(a1) (a2) (a3)] (BA) {};
      \node[box, grp, fit=(g1) (g2) (g3)] (BG) {};
      \node[box, rst, fit=(r1) (r2) (r3)] (BR) {};
    \end{scope}
    \node[above=0.05cm of BA] {$\Ganc$};
    \node[left=0.05cm of BG] {$G$};
    \node[right=0.05cm of BR] {$\Grest$};
    \draw[meta] (BA) -- (BG);
    \draw[meta] (BA) -- (BR);
    \draw[meta] (BG) -- (BR);
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
Write the joint distribution as the product of the factors
$p(v \mid \text{parents of } v)$, and split it into two products:
\begin{align*}
  p(G)
  &= \sum_{\Ganc} \sum_{\Grest}
     \prod_{v \in G \cup \Ganc} p(v \mid \text{parents of } v)
     \prod_{r \in \Grest} p(r \mid \text{parents of } r) \\
  &= \sum_{\Ganc} \prod_{v \in G \cup \Ganc} p(v \mid \text{parents of } v)
     \sum_{\Grest} \prod_{r \in \Grest} p(r \mid \text{parents of } r)
    && \text{(constants out)} \\
  &= \sum_{\Ganc} \prod_{v \in G \cup \Ganc} p(v \mid \text{parents of } v)
    && \text{(sum is 1)}.
\end{align*}
The first product does not contain $\Grest$, because the parents of $G \cup
\Ganc$ are in $G \cup \Ganc$. The last sum is 1: sum first over a variable
of $\Grest$ without children in $\Grest$. Its factor sums to 1, and no other
factor contains it. Then repeat.

For a query $Q$ and an observation $E$, find $p(Q, E)$ and $p(E)$ with the
algorithm, then divide:
\[
  p(Q \mid E) = \frac{p(Q, E)}{p(E)}.
\]

\solpart{Solution}
The tables give $p(X = 1 \mid \text{parents})$ for each node $X$, and
$p(X = 0 \mid \text{parents}) = 1 - p(X = 1 \mid \text{parents})$.

\emph{Joint distribution.} Apply the chain rule one variable at a time,
from the children to the parents. A missing arrow means no direct
influence: given its parents, a node does not depend on the other nodes in
the condition.
\begin{align*}
  p(P, D, A, H, T)
  &= p(P, D, A, H) \, p(T \mid P, D, A, H)
   && \text{(chain rule)} \\
  &= p(P, D, A, H) \, p(T \mid A)
   && \text{(parent of $T$: $A$)} \\
  p(P, D, A, H)
  &= p(P, D, A) \, p(H \mid P, D, A)
   && \text{(chain rule)} \\
  &= p(P, D, A) \, p(H \mid P)
   && \text{(parent of $H$: $P$)} \\
  p(P, D, A)
  &= p(P, D) \, p(A \mid P, D)
   && \text{(chain rule; parents of $A$: $P$, $D$)} \\
  p(P, D)
  &= p(P) \, p(D \mid P)
   && \text{(chain rule)} \\
  &= p(P) \, p(D)
   && \text{($D$ has no parents)}
\end{align*}
Thus
\[
  p(P, D, A, H, T)
  = p(P) \, p(D) \, p(A \mid P, D) \, p(H \mid P) \, p(T \mid A).
\]

\solsubproblem{(a) Find $p(P = 1 \mid H = 1)$.}

\solstep{Step 1. Write the goal as a ratio.}
\[
  p(P = 1 \mid H = 1) = \frac{p(P = 1, H = 1)}{p(H = 1)}.
\]

\solstep{Step 2. Find the numerator: $G = \{P, H\}$, $\Ganc = \varnothing$.}
\begin{center}
  \groupgraph{grp}{rst}{grp}{rst}{rst}
\end{center}
\[
  p(P = 1, H = 1)
  = p(P = 1) \, p(H = 1 \mid P = 1)
  = 0.2 \cdot 0.9
  = 0.18.
\]

\solstep{Step 3. Find the denominator: $G = \{H\}$, $\Ganc = \{P\}$.}
\begin{center}
  \groupgraph{anc}{rst}{grp}{rst}{rst}
\end{center}
\begin{align*}
  p(H = 1)
  &= \sum_{p} p(P = p) \, p(H = 1 \mid P = p) \\
  &= 0.2 \cdot 0.9 + (1 - 0.2) \cdot 0.2
   = 0.18 + 0.16
   = 0.34.
\end{align*}

\solstep{Step 4. Divide.}
\[
  p(P = 1 \mid H = 1)
  = \frac{0.18}{0.34}
  = \frac{9}{17}
  \approx 0.529.
\]

\solsubproblem{(b) Find $p(P = 1 \mid T = 1)$.}

\solstep{Step 1. Write the goal as a ratio.}
\[
  p(P = 1 \mid T = 1) = \frac{p(P = 1, T = 1)}{p(T = 1)}.
\]

\solstep{Step 2. Find the numerator: $G = \{P, T\}$, $\Ganc = \{A, D\}$.}
\begin{center}
  \groupgraph{grp}{anc}{rst}{anc}{grp}
\end{center}
\begin{align*}
  &p(P = 1, T = 1) \\
  &= \sum_{a} \sum_{d} p(P = 1) \, p(D = d) \, p(A = a \mid P = 1, D = d) \,
     p(T = 1 \mid A = a)
    && \text{(algorithm)} \\
  &= p(P = 1) \sum_{a} p(T = 1 \mid A = a) \sum_{d}
     p(A = a \mid P = 1, D = d) \, p(D = d)
    && \text{(constants out)} \\
  &= 0.2 \, \bigl[ 0.95 \, \bigl(0.999 \cdot 0.4 + 0.9 \cdot (1 - 0.4)\bigr)
    && \text{($a = 1$, $a = 0$)} \\
  &\qquad + 0.5 \, \bigl((1 - 0.999) \cdot 0.4 + (1 - 0.9) \cdot (1 - 0.4)\bigr)
     \bigr] \\
  &= 0.2 \, [0.95 \cdot 0.9396 + 0.5 \cdot 0.0604] \\
  &= 0.2 \, [0.89262 + 0.0302]
   = 0.2 \cdot 0.92282
   = 0.184564.
\end{align*}

\solstep{Step 3. Find the denominator: $G = \{T\}$, $\Ganc = \{P, A, D\}$.}
\begin{center}
  \groupgraph{anc}{anc}{rst}{anc}{grp}
\end{center}
\begin{align*}
  &p(T = 1) \\
  &= \sum_{p} \sum_{a} \sum_{d} p(P = p) \, p(D = d) \,
     p(A = a \mid P = p, D = d) \, p(T = 1 \mid A = a)
    && \text{(algorithm)} \\
  &= \sum_{p} p(P = p) \sum_{a} p(T = 1 \mid A = a) \sum_{d}
     p(A = a \mid P = p, D = d) \, p(D = d)
    && \text{(constants out)} \\
  &= 0.184564 + (1 - 0.2) \, \bigl[ 0.95 \, (0.9 \cdot 0.4 + 0.01 \cdot 0.6)
    && \text{($p = 1$: Step 2)} \\
  &\qquad + 0.5 \, \bigl((1 - 0.9) \cdot 0.4 + (1 - 0.01) \cdot 0.6\bigr) \bigr] \\
  &= 0.184564 + 0.8 \, [0.95 \cdot 0.366 + 0.5 \cdot 0.634] \\
  &= 0.184564 + 0.8 \, [0.3477 + 0.317]
   = 0.184564 + 0.8 \cdot 0.6647 \\
  &= 0.184564 + 0.53176
   = 0.716324.
\end{align*}

\solstep{Step 4. Divide.}
\[
  p(P = 1 \mid T = 1)
  = \frac{0.184564}{0.716324}
  \approx 0.258.
\]

\solsubproblem{(c) Find $p(P = 1 \mid T = 1, H = 1)$.}

\solstep{Step 1. Write the goal as a ratio.}
\[
  p(P = 1 \mid T = 1, H = 1)
  = \frac{p(P = 1, H = 1, T = 1)}{p(H = 1, T = 1)}.
\]

\solstep{Step 2. Find the numerator: $G = \{P, H, T\}$, $\Ganc = \{A, D\}$.}
\begin{center}
  \groupgraph{grp}{anc}{grp}{anc}{grp}
\end{center}
\begin{align*}
  &p(P = 1, H = 1, T = 1) \\
  &= \sum_{a} \sum_{d} p(P = 1) \, p(D = d) \, p(A = a \mid P = 1, D = d)
    && \text{(algorithm)} \\
  &\qquad \cdot p(H = 1 \mid P = 1) \, p(T = 1 \mid A = a) \\
  &= p(H = 1 \mid P = 1)
    && \text{(constants out)} \\
  &\qquad \cdot p(P = 1) \sum_{a} p(T = 1 \mid A = a)
     \sum_{d} p(A = a \mid P = 1, D = d) \, p(D = d) \\
  &= 0.9 \cdot 0.184564
    && \text{((b), Step 2)} \\
  &= 0.1661076.
\end{align*}

\solstep{Step 3. Find the denominator: $G = \{H, T\}$, $\Ganc = \{P, A, D\}$.}
\begin{center}
  \groupgraph{anc}{anc}{grp}{anc}{grp}
\end{center}
\begin{align*}
  &p(H = 1, T = 1) \\
  &= \sum_{p} \sum_{a} \sum_{d} p(P = p) \, p(D = d) \,
     p(A = a \mid P = p, D = d)
    && \text{(algorithm)} \\
  &\qquad \cdot p(H = 1 \mid P = p) \, p(T = 1 \mid A = a) \\
  &= \sum_{p} p(H = 1 \mid P = p)
    && \text{(constants out)} \\
  &\qquad \cdot p(P = p) \sum_{a} p(T = 1 \mid A = a)
     \sum_{d} p(A = a \mid P = p, D = d) \, p(D = d) \\
  &= 0.9 \cdot 0.184564 + 0.2 \cdot 0.53176
    && \text{((b), Step 3)} \\
  &= 0.1661076 + 0.106352
   = 0.2724596.
\end{align*}

\solstep{Step 4. Divide.}
\[
  p(P = 1 \mid T = 1, H = 1)
  = \frac{0.1661076}{0.2724596}
  \approx 0.610.
\]

\solpart{Conclusion}
\[
  \underbrace{0.2}_{\text{prior}}
  \;\to\;
  \underbrace{0.529}_{H = 1}, \quad
  \underbrace{0.258}_{T = 1}, \quad
  \underbrace{0.610}_{H = 1,\, T = 1}.
\]
An unhappy teacher is weak evidence: without the party, the teacher is
also often unhappy ($0.6647$ in Step~3 of (b)). Two observations together give the strongest evidence.
\end{solution}
